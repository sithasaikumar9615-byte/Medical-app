# GLP-1 Receptor Agonists: How They Manage Blood Sugar and Help With Weight Loss

## Who this guide is for

This guide is written for adults who have been told they have type 2 diabetes, prediabetes, or obesity, and for the people who care for them. It explains, in everyday language, what GLP-1 receptor agonists are, how they work in the body, what they do well, what they do not do, and what to look out for if you or a family member is taking one. The aim is to help you have a better conversation with your own clinician, not to replace that conversation.

> This guide is general health education. It is not a substitute for personalised medical advice. Talk to your physician, pharmacist, or qualified diabetes educator before starting, stopping, or changing any medication.

## What is GLP-1?

GLP-1 stands for "glucagon-like peptide-1". It is a small hormone that your own body makes. It is released mostly from a type of cell called the L-cell, which sits in the lining of the lower small intestine and the colon. The trigger is simple: when food, especially carbohydrate and fat, reaches the gut after a meal, the L-cells fire and put GLP-1 into the bloodstream within minutes [1][2].

GLP-1 belongs to a family of hormones called incretins. The "incretin effect" describes a long-known observation: if a person swallows a sugary drink, blood glucose rises and the pancreas releases a certain amount of insulin in response. If the same amount of glucose is given directly into a vein, bypassing the gut, the insulin response is much smaller. The difference comes from gut hormones, mostly GLP-1 and a related hormone called GIP, that quietly tell the pancreas, "food is on the way, get ready" [1][3]. In healthy people this incretin effect accounts for a meaningful share of the insulin released after a meal. In type 2 diabetes the incretin effect is blunted, which is one of several reasons after-meal glucose stays high [3].

A useful image is a traffic signal. When a meal arrives, GLP-1 acts like a green light to the pancreas, telling it to release insulin only when there is sugar on the road, and a red light to the liver and the alpha cells of the pancreas, telling them to stop sending more glucose into the bloodstream when none is needed [1]. The body's own GLP-1 has one big practical limitation: an enzyme called dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) breaks it down within about two minutes, so its action is brief [1][2]. This short half-life is why simply infusing natural GLP-1 was never a practical treatment, and why decades of pharmaceutical research went into finding longer-lasting molecules that the body's enzymes do not destroy as quickly [1][2].

The receptors that GLP-1 binds to are not only in the pancreas. They are also present on cells in the stomach, on certain neurons in the hypothalamus and brainstem, and in the heart, kidney, and blood-vessel lining [1][2]. This wider distribution explains why a drug designed to mimic GLP-1 can have effects beyond blood sugar, including on appetite, on stomach emptying, and possibly on cardiovascular tissues [1][4][5].

## What are GLP-1 receptor agonists?

A GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) is a man-made medicine that copies the action of the natural GLP-1 hormone but is designed to last much longer. The molecule has been altered so that DPP-4 cannot easily break it down, so its effect can stretch from many hours to a full week, depending on the specific drug [1][2]. Most of these medicines are given as a small injection just under the skin, with a thin pen-style needle that patients learn to use at home. One of them, oral semaglutide, is a daily tablet that uses a special absorption helper to survive the stomach [17].

The medicines in this class that you are most likely to hear about, listed by their generic name and the most common brand names, are:

- **Exenatide** — sold as **Byetta** (twice-daily injection) and **Bydureon** (once-weekly injection), one of the earliest agents in this class [21][22].
- **Liraglutide** — sold as **Victoza** for type 2 diabetes and as **Saxenda** for chronic weight management. It is given as a once-daily injection [18][19].
- **Dulaglutide** — sold as **Trulicity**. A once-weekly injection used for type 2 diabetes [20].
- **Semaglutide** — sold as **Ozempic** for type 2 diabetes (once-weekly injection), as **Wegovy** for chronic weight management (once-weekly injection at higher doses), and as **Rybelsus** as a once-daily tablet for type 2 diabetes [15][16][17].
- **Lixisenatide** — sold as **Adlyxin** (once-daily injection). It is less widely used today but is still in some treatment guidelines [25].

A newer drug, **tirzepatide**, sold as **Mounjaro** for type 2 diabetes and **Zepbound** for chronic weight management, is often discussed in the same conversation as the GLP-1 RAs because it acts on the same body system. It is more accurate to call tirzepatide a dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist: it mimics two incretin hormones at once, not only GLP-1 [10][11][23][24]. Trials and prescribing labels treat it as a closely related but distinct class.

These medicines differ in how often they are taken (twice-daily, once-daily, or once-weekly), in how strongly they affect blood sugar and weight, and in which conditions a regulator has approved them for. They share the same core idea: keep the GLP-1 signal alive for longer than the body could on its own [1][2].

It is worth knowing that the same generic drug can have two different brand names because it is approved at different doses for different conditions. Liraglutide at the lower 1.8 mg daily dose is sold as Victoza for type 2 diabetes; the same molecule at 3.0 mg daily is sold as Saxenda for chronic weight management [18][19]. Likewise, semaglutide is sold as Ozempic at doses up to about 2.0 mg weekly for type 2 diabetes and as Wegovy at 2.4 mg weekly for chronic weight management [15][16]. Tirzepatide is Mounjaro for type 2 diabetes and Zepbound for chronic weight management, with overlapping dose ranges [23][24]. The brand a patient receives depends on what the prescription is being written for, on local availability, and, in many countries, on what the insurance or national health system will pay for.

## How they lower blood sugar (mechanism)

GLP-1 receptor agonists pull on four levers at once. None of them, on its own, would be enough to control diabetes; together they make a meaningful difference [1][2][3].

**1. Glucose-dependent insulin release from beta cells.** GLP-1 receptors sit on the beta cells of the pancreas, the cells that make insulin. When the receptor is switched on, the beta cell releases more insulin, but only when blood glucose is already rising [1][3]. This is the most important safety feature of the class. If your blood sugar is normal, the medicine does not push insulin out anyway. That is why a GLP-1 RA, used by itself, rarely causes the dangerous low blood sugar seen with insulin or sulfonylurea tablets [12][15][18].

**2. Suppression of inappropriate glucagon release from alpha cells.** Right next to the beta cells are the alpha cells, which make a hormone called glucagon. Glucagon is the opposite of insulin: it tells the liver to dump stored sugar into the blood. In type 2 diabetes the alpha cells are often poorly regulated and keep releasing glucagon at the wrong times, including after a meal, which makes blood sugar rise even higher [1][3]. GLP-1 dampens this inappropriate glucagon release, again in a glucose-dependent way [1].

**3. Slowed gastric emptying.** The stomach normally pushes a meal into the small intestine in waves. GLP-1 slows that process, so the carbohydrate part of the meal trickles into the gut over a longer time rather than arriving as a flood [1][2][15]. The peak in blood glucose after eating is therefore lower and flatter. This is the main reason a person on a GLP-1 RA often notices that the same meal that used to spike their finger-prick reading by a large amount now produces a smaller bump.

**4. Reduced glucose output from the liver.** Once insulin is more available and glucagon is more controlled, the liver, which had been quietly releasing too much sugar between meals and overnight, slows that release [1][3]. This is a downstream consequence of the first two effects rather than a separate direct action, but it explains why fasting glucose readings, taken first thing in the morning, also improve.

In randomised trials, semaglutide and tirzepatide have produced the largest reductions in HbA1c, a three-month average of blood glucose, of any drug class apart from insulin, with average reductions roughly between one and two percentage points compared with placebo, depending on dose and trial design [10][12][15]. The 2024 ADA Standards of Care reflect this and now place GLP-1 RAs and the dual GIP/GLP-1 RA tirzepatide near the top of the second-line options for many adults with type 2 diabetes [12][13].

A point worth repeating: because the insulin-releasing action is glucose-dependent, the risk of severe hypoglycaemia from a GLP-1 RA on its own is low [1][12][15]. The risk goes up if the medicine is combined with insulin or with a sulfonylurea such as glimepiride or glibenclamide, in which case the doctor often lowers the dose of those other drugs at the same time [12][15].

## How they cause weight loss

GLP-1 also acts in the brain. Receptors are present in two regions that govern hunger and the sense of having eaten enough: the arcuate nucleus of the hypothalamus, which contains the POMC and AgRP neurons that set baseline appetite, and the brainstem, especially the area postrema and the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), which receives signals from the gut [1][2]. When a long-acting GLP-1 RA is in the bloodstream, these brain regions act as if a meal has just been eaten, even when one has not [2]. Most patients describe this in everyday terms: smaller portions feel filling, snacking between meals becomes much less appealing, and certain "trigger" foods no longer have the same emotional pull. Researchers call this last effect a reduction in "food reward" [2].

The slowed gastric emptying mentioned earlier also contributes to fullness, because food sits in the stomach for longer [1][16]. The combined result is a smaller daily energy intake without the constant willpower battle that diet programmes alone often require.

The size of the effect has been measured in large clinical trials. In the STEP 1 trial of once-weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg for chronic weight management, adults with overweight or obesity but without diabetes lost on average about 14.9% of their starting body weight after 68 weeks of treatment plus lifestyle support, compared with about 2.4% in the placebo group [7]. In SURMOUNT-1, the equivalent trial of tirzepatide for obesity, the highest dose produced an average loss of around 20.9% of starting body weight at 72 weeks [11]. STEP 8 directly compared once-weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg with daily liraglutide 3.0 mg and found greater weight loss with semaglutide [9].

The harder, less publicised finding from STEP 4 is what happens when the drug is stopped. Participants who had lost weight on semaglutide and then switched to placebo regained roughly two-thirds of the weight they had lost during the next year [8]. This does not mean the medicine "failed". It means GLP-1 RAs are best understood as a long-term treatment for a long-term condition, similar to medicines for blood pressure, rather than a short course that fixes the problem permanently. Any decision about stopping or pausing the drug should be made with the prescribing clinician [8][12].

## Cardiovascular and kidney signals

For many years, diabetes drugs were judged only by whether they lowered blood sugar. After 2008 regulators began asking for large outcome trials that count heart attacks, strokes, and deaths from cardiovascular causes. The standard composite is called major adverse cardiovascular events, or MACE: cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and non-fatal stroke. Several GLP-1 RAs have now been studied this way [12][13].

The LEADER trial of liraglutide enrolled adults with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk and showed a statistically significant reduction in MACE compared with placebo over a median of 3.8 years [4]. SUSTAIN-6 tested injectable semaglutide in a similar population and also showed a reduction in MACE, although it was a smaller and shorter trial [5]. REWIND tested dulaglutide in a broader group, including many people without prior cardiovascular disease, and again found a reduction in MACE [6]. The size of these effects is generally moderate but consistent across trials, which is why the 2024 ADA Standards of Care and the 2022 ADA/EASD consensus report recommend a GLP-1 RA with proven cardiovascular benefit when an adult with type 2 diabetes also has established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or is at high risk for it [12][13].

The kidney signals have been studied as secondary outcomes. In LEADER, SUSTAIN-6, and REWIND, the composite kidney outcome (which usually combines new persistent macroalbuminuria, doubling of serum creatinine, kidney failure, and death from kidney causes) was reduced, mostly because of fewer cases of new macroalbuminuria [4][5][6]. The effect on harder kidney endpoints, such as needing dialysis, has been less clearly demonstrated for the GLP-1 RA class than it has been for the SGLT2 inhibitor class, which is a different family of diabetes drugs. The current guideline position is that a GLP-1 RA is a reasonable choice when a patient has chronic kidney disease and additional cardiovascular risk, although an SGLT2 inhibitor is usually preferred specifically for kidney protection in type 2 diabetes [12][13].

The honest summary is this: the cardiovascular and kidney benefits seen with GLP-1 RAs are real, were demonstrated in large randomised trials, and are reflected in the major guidelines, but they are not magic. The trials enrolled adults at high risk and showed modest absolute reductions in events. They do not turn a person without diabetes into someone who cannot have a heart attack [4][5][6][12].

## Common side effects and how to manage them

Most of the side effects of GLP-1 RAs come from the gut. The slowing of gastric emptying that helps blood sugar control also means that, in the early weeks, food sits longer in the stomach than the body is used to. The most common complaints are nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and constipation [15][16][20]. In the major trials roughly one in four to one in three participants reported nausea at some point, although it was usually mild and most often resolved over the first few weeks of treatment [7][11][15].

Three practical strategies, used by clinicians and supported by the prescribing labels, make a real difference [12][15][16]:

- **Start low and titrate slowly.** Every drug in this class begins at a small starting dose and goes up in steps over weeks or months. Skipping ahead to the target dose almost always produces more nausea. If side effects appear, the dose can be held at the current step for longer rather than pushed up [15][16].
- **Eat smaller meals and stop sooner.** Because the stomach now empties more slowly, the same plate that used to feel comfortable can feel overfull. Eating slowly, putting cutlery down between bites, and finishing earlier are simple changes that reduce nausea.
- **Stay hydrated and watch for constipation.** Drink water through the day, include fibre, and stay active. If constipation persists, ask the pharmacist or doctor about a gentle laxative.

The labels also note increased reports of gallbladder events, including gallstones and, less commonly, inflammation of the gallbladder, particularly with rapid weight loss [16][19]. Persistent severe right-upper-abdominal pain, especially with fever or jaundice, should prompt urgent medical review.

Injection-site reactions, such as redness, mild itching, or a small lump at the site of the weekly injection, are usually self-limiting and rarely cause stopping of the drug [15][20]. Rotating injection sites and following the pen instructions reduce these.

A small number of patients describe a strong taste change, fatigue, or burping in the early weeks. These usually settle. Severe, persistent, or unusual symptoms should never be self-managed; speak to the prescriber.

It is also worth being aware of a less common but important pattern. Because GLP-1 RAs slow gastric emptying, food and tablets may move through the stomach more slowly than the body is used to. Patients who take many oral medicines, especially medicines whose absorption is sensitive to timing, should mention this to their pharmacist. The labels recommend caution when GLP-1 RAs are co-prescribed with oral drugs that have a narrow therapeutic window, such as warfarin, although for most patients no formal dose change is needed [15][20]. The labels also note that, in people undergoing planned anaesthesia, the slowed stomach emptying may be relevant to fasting before surgery; this has been the subject of recent professional society guidance and is worth raising with the surgical team in advance [15][16].

## Serious warnings and contraindications

Three areas are highlighted in bold in the FDA prescribing information of most drugs in this class and deserve attention [15][16][18][19][20][22][23][24].

**1. Thyroid C-cell tumours and MEN-2.** The US prescribing labels of liraglutide, dulaglutide, semaglutide, exenatide extended-release, and tirzepatide carry a boxed warning. In rodent studies these drugs caused medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) and C-cell tumours. Whether the same risk exists in humans is unclear, but as a precaution the labels say the drugs are contraindicated in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma and in people with multiple endocrine neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN-2) [15][16][18][19][20][22][23][24]. Patients should know to report any unexplained neck mass, hoarseness, or difficulty swallowing.

**2. Pancreatitis.** Cases of acute pancreatitis, including a small number of fatal and haemorrhagic cases, have been reported in people taking GLP-1 RAs in post-marketing surveillance, and most labels in the class mention this signal [15][16][18][22]. Patients should be told to seek urgent care for severe, persistent abdominal pain that radiates to the back, especially if accompanied by vomiting. The drug is usually stopped if pancreatitis is confirmed.

**3. Hypoglycaemia when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas.** As mentioned earlier, the GLP-1 RA itself rarely causes low blood sugar. The combination with insulin or with a sulfonylurea (such as glimepiride, gliclazide, or glibenclamide) can cause hypoglycaemia, sometimes severe [12][15]. The prescribing clinician will often reduce the dose of the partner drug when starting a GLP-1 RA. Patients should know the symptoms of low blood sugar (sweating, shaking, hunger, confusion, headache) and how to treat it [12].

Other practical points from the labels: pregnancy is generally a reason to stop these drugs, because there is not enough human safety data and animal studies have shown harm at high doses [15][16][18]. Women who are planning a pregnancy or who become pregnant should discuss alternatives with their doctor. Severe gastroparesis (a condition in which the stomach already empties very slowly) is usually a reason to avoid this class, because the drug would worsen it [15][16]. People with severe, active gallbladder disease should also discuss the risks before starting [16][19].

## Who typically gets prescribed a GLP-1 RA

In most national guidelines, metformin remains the first medicine offered to most adults with type 2 diabetes when lifestyle change alone is not enough [12][13][14]. After that, the choice of the next drug depends on the person, not only on the HbA1c [12][13].

The 2024 American Diabetes Association Standards of Care recommend a GLP-1 RA, or in some situations the dual GIP/GLP-1 RA tirzepatide, when any of the following is a priority [12]:

- The patient has, or is at high risk for, established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
- The patient has heart failure.
- The patient has chronic kidney disease.
- Weight management is a meaningful goal of treatment, including in adults with type 2 diabetes who have a high body weight.

The 2022 ADA/EASD consensus report on the management of hyperglycaemia in type 2 diabetes makes a similar argument and frames the choice as person-centred: the clinician asks what matters most for this individual, and then chooses the drug whose evidence best matches that priority [13]. In the United Kingdom, the NICE guideline NG28 on type 2 diabetes in adults gives a stepped pathway and lists GLP-1 RAs as an option in specific situations, particularly for people whose body mass index is high and who have not reached glucose targets on oral drugs [14]. The exact eligibility criteria are stricter in the UK pathway than in the US one, and they may change as guidelines are updated.

For chronic weight management without diabetes, the picture is different. Liraglutide (Saxenda), semaglutide (Wegovy), and tirzepatide (Zepbound) are approved by the US FDA, and similar approvals exist in other countries, for adults with obesity (BMI 30 or above) or with overweight (BMI 27 or above) and at least one weight-related condition such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, or dyslipidaemia [16][19][24]. These are approved as additions to a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity, not as a replacement for them.

A practical note on access. In several countries the demand for these medicines, particularly semaglutide and tirzepatide, has at times outstripped supply. Patients have sometimes had to switch between brands or doses because of shortages, and clinicians have had to choose alternative agents. Anyone who has been started on a GLP-1 RA should ask the prescribing clinician what to do if the usual brand or dose is not available at the pharmacy, and should be cautious of products sold outside the regulated supply chain. Counterfeit semaglutide pens, often advertised online, have been reported in several countries and can contain unknown ingredients or incorrect doses [15][16].

## What this is NOT

It helps to be honest about what GLP-1 RAs are and are not.

They are not a magic bullet. The trials with the largest weight loss numbers also included structured lifestyle support, regular dietary counselling, and physical activity goals [7][11]. The drug helps the diet stick. It does not replace the diet.

They are not a substitute for moving more, sleeping enough, and eating in a way you can sustain. Muscle mass, cardiovascular fitness, and mental health respond to behaviour, not to a weekly injection. Most experts recommend resistance training and adequate protein intake during treatment to preserve muscle mass during weight loss [12][13].

They are not a short course. Type 2 diabetes and obesity are chronic conditions. The STEP 4 trial showed that, when semaglutide was stopped after a year of weight loss, most of that weight returned within the next year [8]. This is the same pattern seen when blood pressure medicines are stopped: the disease is still there, the medicine simply was not in the body to control it any longer.

They are not first-line in type 1 diabetes. People with type 1 diabetes do not make insulin and need insulin replacement; the GLP-1 RA class is not approved as a stand-alone treatment for type 1 diabetes [12]. Limited research has explored their use as an add-on in selected adults with type 1 diabetes, but this is outside their licensed use in most countries.

They are not the right choice for everyone. The contraindications above (medullary thyroid carcinoma history, MEN-2, severe gastroparesis, pregnancy) really do mean "not for this patient". Online demand and marketing should not override these limits.

They are also not a substitute for the rest of diabetes care. Adults with type 2 diabetes still need their blood pressure managed, their cholesterol assessed and treated where appropriate, their feet checked once a year, their eyes screened for retinopathy, and their kidneys monitored with simple blood and urine tests [12][13][14]. A GLP-1 RA can do a lot of useful work on its own, but it does not replace the rest of the routine that prevents long-term complications.

## Talking to your doctor

If you and your doctor are considering a GLP-1 RA, useful questions to ask include:

- What is the main goal you and I are aiming for: lower HbA1c, weight loss, lower cardiovascular risk, kidney protection, or some combination?
- Which drug in the class is right for me, and why this one rather than another?
- How will we titrate the dose, and what side effects should I expect in the first month?
- Do any of my current medicines, especially insulin or sulfonylureas, need to be reduced when I start this?
- What symptoms should make me stop the drug and call you straight away?
- How long do you expect me to stay on this medicine, and what is the plan if I tolerate it well, or if I do not?
- What lifestyle changes do you most want me to focus on alongside the drug?
- How will the cost and the supply of this medicine work in my situation?

Bring a written list. Take notes during the appointment. Ask the pharmacist to walk you through the injection device the first time you fill the prescription.

It can also help to keep a simple home log during the first few months. Patients who note their weekly weight, any side effects, the dose they are on, and one or two finger-prick blood-sugar readings around meals find it much easier to have a productive conversation at follow-up visits. The same log makes it easier for the clinician to decide whether to push the dose up to the next step, hold at the current step, or switch to a different agent. Keep the log short. A single line a day is enough.
