## 1. MISSION, AUDIENCE, BRAND VOICE

**Your Mission Statement (pin to your studio wall):**
> "I help Indian patients and their families understand diabetes through evidence — so they can choose lifestyle, medication and reversal pathways without fear or false promises."

**Primary Audience:**
- Adults 28–60, newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes or prediabetes
- Family members of patients with Type 1 diabetes
- People at high risk: BMI ≥ 25, family history, PCOS, gestational diabetes history
- Pharmacists, nursing staff, junior doctors who refer to your channel

**Brand Pillars:**
1. **Evidence over emotion** — every claim cites a study or guideline.
2. **Empathy first** — talk *with* people, not *down to* them.
3. **Pharmacology you can feel** — explain drugs by what they do in the body, not by brand name.
4. **Indian context** — rice, roti, millets, chai, festivals, Ramadan fasting, shift workers.
5. **Honest about reversal** — yes for many T2D, never for T1D.

**Brand Voice Rules:**
- Speak in 8th-grade English / simple Hindi-English blend.
- Never say "cure" — say "remission" and explain why.
- Never name and shame other influencers — debunk the *idea*, not the *person*.
- Always end with one specific action the viewer can do today.

## 2. COPYRIGHT-SAFE PRODUCTION GUIDE

This is the single most important section. Follow this and you will not get an Instagram or YouTube strike.

### 2.1 Music
- **Do not** use trending Bollywood, Spotify, or commercial tracks. Even 5 seconds = strike.
- **Use** Instagram's in-app Reels music library *only* if you are on a personal/creator account (not business — business accounts have a heavily restricted catalogue).
- **For YouTube** use only:
  - YouTube Audio Library (free, monetisation-safe)
  - Pixabay Music (CC0)
  - Uppbeat (free tier with attribution)
  - Epidemic Sound (paid subscription) or Artlist (paid subscription) if you can afford it
- Save licence receipts/screenshots in a folder called `LICENCES/`.

### 2.2 Stock video, B-roll and images
Use only these sources, all free for commercial use, no attribution required (unless noted):
- **Pexels** (pexels.com) — video + photo
- **Pixabay** (pixabay.com) — video + photo + illustrations
- **Unsplash** (unsplash.com) — photo
- **Mixkit** (mixkit.co) — video
- **Coverr** (coverr.co) — video
- **Storyblocks** (paid, unlimited)
- For medical illustrations: **SMART Servier Medical Art** (smart.servier.com) — CC-BY 3.0 (give credit in description)
- For anatomy: **BioRender** (paid, but the gold standard for science creators) or **Anatomy & Physiology** OpenStax images (CC-BY 4.0)

### 2.3 What you must build yourself
- Your face on camera (your strongest copyright shield is *you*).
- All text overlays, motion graphics, lower-thirds — make in **Canva Pro** or **CapCut** (use only their licensed assets).
- All flowcharts and tables — recreate the ones in Section 8 of this document in Canva. The flowcharts in this PDF are *plain-text descriptions* you reproduce yourself.

### 2.4 Citing journal images
- A figure from a peer-reviewed paper is **not** automatically free to use. Even if the paper is open-access, check the licence (CC-BY is reusable with credit; "all rights reserved" is not).
- Safest rule: **read the paper, redraw the figure yourself in Canva**, cite the paper in the description.

### 2.5 Brand & product mentions
- Never name a specific brand of medicine in a way that looks promotional ("XYZ tablet is best"). Say the **molecule** ("metformin", "empagliflozin").
- Disclose any sponsorship in the first 3 seconds of the video and in the caption with #ad #sponsored.

### 2.6 Medical disclaimer (paste in every caption)
> *Disclaimer: This content is for general education only and is not a substitute for personalised medical advice. Please consult your physician, pharmacist or diabetes educator before changing your diet, exercise or medication.*

## DAY 2 — What is diabetes in one minute

### Hook lines
- "If you can read this sentence, your brain is running on glucose right now. Diabetes is what happens when that delivery system breaks."
- "Diabetes is not a sugar disease. It is an insulin disease. Today's video changes how you see it."
- "Your blood is supposed to have sugar in it. The problem starts when there is too much, for too long."

### Instagram Reel script (75 seconds)

**[0:00–0:05] Hook**
"Diabetes is not a sugar disease. It is an insulin disease."

**[0:05–0:25] The simple version**
"Every cell in your body needs glucose for fuel. Glucose comes from food. To get into the cell, glucose needs a key called insulin, made by your pancreas.³"

**[0:25–0:45] What goes wrong**
"In Type 1 diabetes, the body destroys the insulin factory. In Type 2, the lock on the cell becomes rusty — insulin is there but doesn't work well. Either way, sugar piles up in the blood instead of feeding the cell.⁴"

**[0:45–1:10] Why it matters**
"That extra sugar damages your eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart and feet — slowly, silently, for years before you feel anything.⁵ This is why we treat the *number* before you feel the *symptom*."

**[1:10–1:15] CTA**
"Tomorrow: how glucose and insulin actually talk to each other. Save and follow."

### YouTube script (5 min)

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**
"You eat a roti. Within fifteen minutes, sugar from that roti is in your bloodstream. In a healthy body, it leaves the blood and enters your cells in under two hours. In diabetes, that journey breaks. Today we'll see exactly where it breaks."

**[0:20–1:15] Where glucose comes from**
"Carbohydrates — rice, roti, dal, fruit, sugar, even milk — are broken down into glucose, a single sugar molecule. Your gut absorbs it into the blood. Your liver also makes glucose during fasting. So your blood always has some glucose: between 70 and 100 mg/dL when you wake up, between 100 and 140 after a meal.⁶"

**[1:15–2:15] What insulin does**
"The pancreas, a leaf-shaped organ behind your stomach, has tiny clumps of cells called the islets of Langerhans. Inside the islets, beta-cells make insulin. Insulin is a hormone — a chemical messenger. When glucose rises in the blood after a meal, beta-cells release insulin. Insulin binds to a receptor on the surface of muscle, fat and liver cells, and triggers a transporter called GLUT4 to come to the surface and pull glucose in.⁷ Once glucose is inside the cell, it's burned for energy or stored. Blood glucose comes back down. That's the normal cycle."

**[2:15–3:15] Type 1 — the factory is destroyed**
"In Type 1 diabetes, your own immune system — the same one that fights infections — mistakenly attacks the beta-cells. Over months to years, more than 80% of them are destroyed.⁸ Insulin production crashes. Without insulin, glucose can't enter cells, even though there's plenty of it in the blood. The body starts breaking down fat for fuel, producing ketones, and the patient ends up in diabetic ketoacidosis. Type 1 needs insulin from outside, for life."

**[3:15–4:15] Type 2 — the locks get rusty**
"Type 2 is different. The factory still works at first — sometimes even overworks. The problem is the locks. Muscle, liver and fat cells stop responding properly to insulin; this is called insulin resistance. The pancreas tries to compensate by making more insulin. For years, this hides the problem. Eventually, the beta-cells get tired, and now you have insulin resistance *plus* falling insulin — that's full-blown Type 2 diabetes.⁹"

**[4:15–5:00] Why we care about a number you can't feel**
"Here's the cruel part. You won't feel high blood sugar until it's been high for years. By the time symptoms hit — frequent urination, thirst, weight loss, blurred vision — the damage to small blood vessels has already started.¹⁰ This is why screening matters. This is why treating the number matters. Tomorrow we'll go deeper into the glucose-insulin handshake. See you then."

## DAY 3 — Glucose, insulin, and why your body needs both

### Hook lines
- "Insulin is not the bad guy. Insulin is what keeps you alive after every meal."
- "If insulin disappeared for 24 hours, you would not survive. Today's video shows you why."
- "Your phone needs charging. Your cells need insulin. That's it. That's the lesson."

### Instagram Reel (90 sec)

**[0:00–0:05] Hook**
"Insulin is not the bad guy. Insulin is what keeps you alive after every meal."

**[0:05–0:30] What insulin actually does**
"Three jobs. One — opens muscle and fat cells so glucose can enter. Two — tells the liver to stop making more glucose. Three — tells the body to store extra fuel as glycogen and fat for later.¹¹"

**[0:30–0:55] The myth**
"Social media says 'insulin makes you fat'. Truth — insulin manages the fuel you eat. Eat too much, of course you'll store fat. Insulin is the warehouse manager, not the thief."

**[0:55–1:20] Why diabetics need it**
"In Type 1, you make zero. In late Type 2, you make too little. Without enough insulin, your blood is rich in glucose but your cells are starving. That's why people lose weight in uncontrolled diabetes."

**[1:20–1:30] CTA**
"Tomorrow — meet the pancreas. Save this Reel."

### YouTube (5 min 30 sec)

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**
"There's a viral idea online that insulin is the enemy. That you should reduce insulin to lose weight, to reverse diabetes, to live longer. Today I'll show you why that idea is half right and half dangerously wrong."

**[0:20–1:30] What insulin really does**
"Insulin is a hormone made by the beta cells of the pancreas. Think of it as a postman delivering three messages. Message one, to muscle and fat cells: open your doors, glucose is here. Message two, to the liver: stop pouring out glucose, the meal has arrived. Message three, to the whole body: store the surplus — as glycogen in liver and muscle, and as fat in adipose tissue, for the next fasting period.¹¹ Without these three messages, glucose stays trapped in the blood, and the body literally starves in the middle of plenty."

**[1:30–2:30] The fed state vs the fasting state**
"After a meal, insulin rises. Glucose falls. The body is in 'fed state' — storing energy. Between meals or overnight, insulin falls and another hormone, glucagon, takes over. Glucagon tells the liver to release stored glucose. This back and forth is how your blood sugar stays in the 70-to-140 mg/dL range no matter what you eat. In diabetes, the back and forth breaks. Either insulin doesn't come (Type 1), or it comes but the cells ignore it (Type 2)."¹²

**[2:30–3:30] Why the 'insulin makes you fat' line is misleading**
"Yes, insulin promotes storage. But insulin only acts on what you eat. If you eat 1,500 calories, insulin manages 1,500 calories. The reason people gain weight in modern life is excess calories — especially refined carbohydrates and ultra-processed snacks — not insulin existing. Studies that compare low-carb to low-fat diets, when calories are matched, show very similar weight loss.¹³ The honest summary: insulin is a tool. Cutting refined carbs lowers insulin spikes, which is helpful for many T2D patients. But demonising the hormone itself is wrong."

**[3:30–4:30] What happens without insulin**
"Imagine the postman is gone. Glucose arrives in the blood after a meal but cannot enter cells. The blood sugar climbs to 300, 400, 500 mg/dL. The cells, starving, send signals to the liver to make more glucose — which makes the blood sugar even higher. The body shifts to burning fat for fuel, producing ketones, which acidify the blood. This is diabetic ketoacidosis: nausea, vomiting, deep rapid breathing, confusion, coma. Without insulin, a Type 1 diabetic can be in DKA in less than 24 hours.¹⁴ This is why nobody — no influencer, no diet, no oil — can replace insulin in Type 1."

**[4:30–5:30] What it means for you**
"If you are on insulin and someone tells you to stop because insulin is bad — please ignore them, then call your doctor before you do anything. If you are on metformin or other tablets, none of those tablets are insulin; they help your own pancreas or your own cells use insulin better. Tomorrow we'll go inside the pancreas itself. See you in the next one."

"The islets of Langerhans contain beta cells, which make insulin to *lower* glucose, and alpha cells, which make glucagon to *raise* glucose. They balance you minute by minute.¹⁶"

**[0:55–1:15] What goes wrong**
"In Type 1, the immune system kills beta cells. In Type 2, beta cells get tired and alpha cells start over-secreting glucagon — so you get less insulin AND more glucose dumping from the liver."¹⁷

**[1:15–1:20] CTA**
"Tomorrow: HbA1c — the 3-month memory of your blood. Save this."

### YouTube (5 min)

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**
"This organ weighs less than 100 grams. It is hidden behind your stomach. You cannot feel it. And yet, when it fails, an entire global industry has to keep you alive with injections. Meet the pancreas."

**[0:20–1:15] Anatomy**
"The pancreas sits horizontally in the upper abdomen, behind the stomach, in front of the spine. It has a head — tucked into the curve of the duodenum — a body, and a tail that reaches towards the spleen. Its main duct empties into the small intestine, near the bile duct.¹⁵ It has two completely separate jobs. The exocrine pancreas, which is most of the gland, makes digestive enzymes that break down protein, fat and carbs in your gut. The endocrine pancreas — only 1 to 2% of the organ — is responsible for everything we discuss in diabetes."

**[1:15–2:15] The Islets of Langerhans**
"Scattered through the pancreas are about a million tiny clusters called the Islets of Langerhans, named after the German pathologist who described them in 1869. Each islet contains several hormone-making cell types. Beta cells make insulin and account for about 60-80% of the islet. Alpha cells make glucagon. Delta cells make somatostatin. PP cells make pancreatic polypeptide. Together, they are a miniature endocrine factory.¹⁶"

**[2:15–3:15] How beta cells decide**
"Beta cells have a sensor for glucose. When blood glucose rises, glucose enters the beta cell through GLUT2, gets metabolised, and produces ATP. ATP closes a potassium channel, the cell becomes electrically excited, calcium rushes in, and pre-formed insulin granules fuse with the cell membrane and dump their contents into the blood.¹⁸ This entire process happens within a few minutes of you eating. Sulphonylureas — the older diabetes tablets — work by closing that same potassium channel, forcing the cell to release insulin even if the glucose isn't high enough."

**[3:15–4:15] How alpha cells decide**
"Alpha cells do the opposite. When blood glucose falls — between meals, during exercise, while you sleep — alpha cells secrete glucagon. Glucagon travels to the liver and tells it to break down glycogen, the body's stored sugar, and release glucose into the blood. In Type 2 diabetes, there is a strange and underappreciated problem: alpha cells become inappropriately active even after meals, secreting glucagon when they shouldn't. The liver dumps glucose right when you've just eaten. This is one of the eight defects in DeFronzo's 'Ominous Octet' theory of T2D.¹⁷"

**[4:15–5:00] What this means in practice**
"This anatomy lecture has a practical point. It tells you why we use multiple drugs — because Type 2 diabetes isn't one defect. Metformin works on the liver and muscle. SGLT2 inhibitors work on the kidney. GLP-1 receptor agonists work on the alpha and beta cells, the brain and the gut. Insulin replaces what the beta cell can no longer make. There is no single magic pill — because there is no single broken part. Tomorrow, we'll talk about HbA1c — your blood's three-month report card. See you in the next one."

## DAY 5 — HbA1c: the 3-month memory of your blood

### Hook lines
- "Your blood sugar lies. Your HbA1c doesn't."
- "One blood test tells your doctor what you've been eating for the last three months. Yes, even the cake at your cousin's wedding."

### Instagram Reel (90 sec)

**[0:00–0:05] Hook**
"Your blood sugar lies. Your HbA1c does not."

**[0:05–0:30] What HbA1c is**
"Glucose in your blood sticks to haemoglobin in your red blood cells. Red cells live about 120 days. So measuring how 'sugar-coated' they are tells us your average sugar over 3 months.¹⁹"

**[0:30–0:55] The numbers**
"Below 5.7% is normal. 5.7 to 6.4% is prediabetes. 6.5% or more on two tests is diabetes.²⁰ Each 1% drop in HbA1c reduces small-vessel complications by about 35%.²¹"

**[0:55–1:20] When it lies**
"It can be falsely low if you have anaemia, recent blood loss, or certain haemoglobin variants common in India.²² That's why one test alone is not enough."

**[1:20–1:30] CTA**
"Tomorrow — fasting, post-meal, random sugar — what each really means. Save this Reel."

### YouTube (5 min 30 sec)

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**
"You can fast for 12 hours, drink only water, and walk in for a blood test that says your sugar is fine. And still — you can be diabetic. The single test that catches you is HbA1c. Today we'll explain it like adults."

**[0:20–1:30] What HbA1c actually measures**
"Inside every red blood cell is haemoglobin — the protein that carries oxygen. Glucose floating in your bloodstream slowly attaches itself to haemoglobin in a process called glycation. The longer glucose has been high, and the higher it has been, the more haemoglobin gets glycated. We measure the percentage of haemoglobin that is glycated — that's HbA1c. Because red blood cells live about 120 days, HbA1c reflects average glucose over the past 2 to 3 months.¹⁹"

**[1:30–2:30] Reading the number**
"The American Diabetes Association cut-offs are: below 5.7% is normal, 5.7 to 6.4% is prediabetes, 6.5% or higher on two separate tests confirms diabetes.²⁰ The ICMR uses the same cut-offs in India. There's also an estimated average glucose — eAG — that translates HbA1c into mg/dL: 6% ≈ 126 mg/dL, 7% ≈ 154, 8% ≈ 183, 9% ≈ 212, 10% ≈ 240. So when your doctor says 'HbA1c of 9 is bad', what she means is your average sugar for three months has been over 200."

**[2:30–3:30] Why HbA1c matters more than a single fasting sugar**
"Fasting glucose is one snapshot. After you eat, your sugar can spike to 250 and come back down — and morning fasting will look fine. HbA1c sees those spikes. The UKPDS trial, which followed thousands of T2D patients for over a decade, showed that every 1 percentage-point reduction in HbA1c reduced microvascular complications — eyes, kidneys, nerves — by about 37%, and reduced heart attack risk by about 14%.²¹ So this number is not for your doctor's file. It is for your eyes, your kidneys, and your heart."

**[3:30–4:30] When HbA1c lies**
"HbA1c is not perfect. Anything that changes the lifespan of red blood cells changes the test. If your red cells live shorter — for example in iron-deficiency anaemia after treatment, in haemolytic anaemia, in pregnancy, in chronic kidney disease, after blood loss — your HbA1c will read falsely low. If your red cells live longer — untreated iron deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, alcoholism — your HbA1c can read falsely high.²² India also has a high prevalence of haemoglobin variants like HbE, HbS and beta-thalassaemia trait, especially in eastern and southern states; these can interfere with certain HbA1c assays.²³ This is why we don't diagnose diabetes on HbA1c alone in such patients — we add a fasting sugar or an OGTT."

**[4:30–5:30] How to use this number**
"If your HbA1c is in prediabetes range, you have a window. Lifestyle alone can prevent or delay diabetes by up to 58% over 3 years, as shown in the Diabetes Prevention Program.²⁴ If you are diabetic, the target is usually under 7%, but it depends on your age, complications, and risk of low sugar — a 75-year-old with heart disease should not aim for 6%. Recheck every 3 months if you're adjusting treatment, every 6 months if stable. Tomorrow we'll go even deeper — fasting, post-meal and random sugars, and what they actually tell your doctor. See you then."

## DAY 6 — Fasting, post-meal, random — what each number means

### Hook lines
- "Three different sugar tests. Three different stories. Most patients only know one."
- "If your fasting sugar is fine but you feel sleepy after lunch — your post-meal sugar may be telling on you."

### Instagram Reel (90 sec)

**[0:00–0:05] Hook**
"Three different sugar tests. Three different stories."

**[0:05–0:30] Fasting**
"After 8 hours without food. Tells you what your liver is doing overnight. Normal: 70–99 mg/dL. Diabetes: 126 or above on two tests.²⁰"

**[0:30–0:55] Post-meal (PPBS)**
"Two hours after the first bite. Tells you how your beta cells handle a meal. Normal: under 140. Diabetes: 200 or above.²⁰"

**[0:55–1:15] Random**
"Any time of day. Useful only if very high — 200 or above with classic symptoms means diabetes regardless of meal.²⁰"

**[1:15–1:30] CTA**
"Tomorrow — symptoms most people ignore until it's too late. Save this."

### YouTube (5 min)

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**
"A patient walks into the clinic with a fasting sugar of 95. 'Doctor, I'm fine.' Two hours after lunch he's at 270. He's been undiagnosed for years. This is why we don't trust just one number."

**[0:20–1:20] Fasting plasma glucose (FPG)**
"FPG is measured after at least 8 hours without food — usually first thing in the morning before breakfast. Water is allowed. It tells us what your body, especially your liver, is doing in the absence of food. The liver releases glucose overnight to keep your brain fed; if that release is over-active, fasting sugar will be high. Normal is below 100 mg/dL. Prediabetes — what we call impaired fasting glucose — is 100 to 125. Diabetes is 126 or higher, confirmed on two separate days.²⁰ Errors: chewing gum, sugary mouth-rinse, or even strong stress before the test can push this number up by 10 to 15 points."

**[1:20–2:20] Post-prandial plasma glucose (PPBS / 2hPG)**
"This is measured exactly 2 hours after the start of a normal meal — not 2 hours after the end. It tells us how well the beta cells respond to a real glucose load. Normal under 140 mg/dL. Impaired glucose tolerance: 140 to 199. Diabetes: 200 or higher.²⁰ This number tends to rise *before* fasting glucose does. Many South Asians with normal fasting sugars already have impaired post-meal sugars — what's called isolated post-prandial hyperglycaemia.²⁵ This is why doctors often add an OGTT or HbA1c when the fasting test alone looks fine but the patient is high-risk."

**[2:20–3:20] Random plasma glucose (RPG)**
"This is taken at any time of day, irrespective of meals. It's not used for routine diagnosis because of meal interference, but a single value of 200 mg/dL or higher *with* classic symptoms — excess thirst, frequent urination, weight loss, blurred vision — is sufficient for a diabetes diagnosis without retesting.²⁰ This is the test we use in emergency settings."

**[3:20–4:00] What about glucometer readings at home?**
"A finger-prick glucometer is great for trends, not for diagnosis. Lab plasma readings are about 10 to 12% higher than capillary readings on the same person at the same time, because the meter measures whole blood. Treat your meter as a self-monitoring tool, not as the source of diagnosis. For diagnosis, always go to a NABL-accredited lab.²⁶"

**[4:00–5:00] Putting all three together**
"A complete picture of your glucose has three layers. Fasting tells me about your liver and overnight metabolism. Post-meal tells me about your beta cells' response to food. HbA1c — yesterday's video — gives me the average over 3 months. Each number can be misleading on its own. Together, they paint the truth. Tomorrow we'll talk about the symptoms most people ignore until it's too late. See you then."

**[4:15–5:00] When to see a doctor today**
"If you have any of these — unintentional weight loss greater than 5% of body weight in 3 months, unexplained vomiting, deep rapid breathing, breath that smells like nail-polish-remover, persistent abdominal pain in someone with diabetes, fainting, confusion — go to the emergency department. ## DAY 9 — OGTT: the gold-standard test, explained

### Hook lines
- "75 grams of glucose, two hours, one needle. The test that catches diabetes others miss."
- "If your fasting sugar is normal but your post-meal is high — only the OGTT will catch you."
- "The diabetes test you've heard about but never understood. Demystified in 5 minutes."

### Instagram Reel (75 sec)

**[0:00–0:05] Hook**
"75 grams of glucose, 2 hours, 1 needle — the test that catches diabetes others miss."

**[0:05–0:30] The procedure**
"You come fasted. They draw a blood sample. You drink a glass of water with 75 grams of glucose. Two hours later, they draw a second sample.⁴⁰"

**[0:30–0:55] What it tells**
"Less than 140 — normal. 140 to 199 — impaired glucose tolerance, prediabetes. 200 or more — diabetes.⁴⁰"

**[0:55–1:15] Why it matters**
"Many Indians have normal fasting and high 2-hour values. Without OGTT, they're missed.⁴¹"

**[1:15–1:20] CTA**
"Tomorrow — should you buy a CGM? Save this Reel."

### YouTube (5 min 30 sec)

**[0:00–0:20] Cold open**
"This test has been called the gold standard of diabetes diagnosis for nearly 60 years. It is also the test most patients refuse because they think it'll spike their sugar. Today we'll go through it, exactly."

**[0:20–1:30] What an OGTT is**
"OGTT stands for Oral Glucose Tolerance Test. The patient comes fasting — at least 8 hours, not more than 14. A blood sample is drawn for fasting plasma glucose. The patient then drinks 75 grams of anhydrous glucose dissolved in 250 to 300 mL of water, within 5 minutes. A second blood sample is drawn at exactly 2 hours from the first sip.⁴⁰ For pregnant women, a 100-gram, 3-hour version exists, with samples at 0, 1, 2 and 3 hours, and different cut-offs apply.⁴² For diagnosis of gestational diabetes, the WHO and DIPSI now recommend a single 75-g, 2-hour test."

**[1:30–2:30] Reading the result**
"A 2-hour value below 140 mg/dL is normal. Between 140 and 199, you have impaired glucose tolerance — a form of prediabetes. 200 or higher confirms diabetes — though we typically need a second confirmatory test on a different day, unless symptoms are unmistakable.⁴⁰ Note: the fasting sample also matters. Some people have normal fasting and abnormal 2-hour values — isolated post-prandial hyperglycaemia. Others have abnormal fasting and normal 2-hour — isolated fasting hyperglycaemia. Both are real prediabetes, with different mechanisms."

**[2:30–3:30] Why it catches what other tests miss**
"In the Chennai Urban Rural Epidemiology Study (CURES), about 30% of newly-diagnosed diabetics had a normal fasting glucose but abnormal OGTT.⁴¹ This pattern is especially common in Indians because of early beta-cell defects in the post-meal phase. If we only do fasting tests, we miss these patients for years — during which their eyes and kidneys are quietly damaged."

**[3:30–4:30] Common myths**
"Myth one: 'The OGTT will spike my sugar dangerously.' Reality: 75 g of glucose is roughly equivalent to a Coke or a sweet lassi; it is safe in non-pregnant adults without symptoms of severe hyperglycaemia. Do not do an OGTT if your fasting glucose is already over 250 mg/dL, you are vomiting, or you have an acute illness — the test isn't needed there. Myth two: 'I should drink it slowly.' No — within 5 minutes, exactly. Myth three: 'I can skip dinner before the test.' No, eat normally for the 3 days before so your body is not glucose-deprived; that can falsely elevate the result."

**[4:30–5:30] When OGTT is preferred**
"OGTT is preferred when fasting and HbA1c don't agree, when haemoglobin variants make HbA1c unreliable, in pregnancy screening, in suspected reactive hypoglycaemia, in suspected acromegaly suppression test, and in many research settings. It is the most accurate single test we have, even today.⁴³ The downside — it takes 2 hours and patients hate it. So we use it when we need it, not for routine annual screening. "Type 2 is metabolic, not autoimmune. The beta cells are alive — they're just exhausted. Three things go wrong, sometimes for decades, before the diagnosis. Three — fat accumulates inside the pancreas and liver, in what Roy Taylor calls the twin-cycle hypothesis.⁵¹ This causes lipotoxicity, further damaging beta cells. "Type 1 treatment: insulin from outside, every day, for life. There is no oral pill that replaces insulin in T1D. New drugs like teplizumab can delay onset in pre-clinical T1D, but they don't cure established disease.⁵² Type 2 treatment: a stairway. Lifestyle first. Then metformin. **[3:45–4:45] Reversal — the most important difference**

## DAY 52 — Sulphonylureas: useful but old

## DAY 59 — Insulin pens and pumps: practical know-how

**Hooks:** *"Wrong injection technique = 30% of your dose wasted in the wrong tissue."* / *"Site rotation is not optional. Lipohypertrophy is real."*

## DAY 95 — Apple cider vinegar — overhyped or useful?
