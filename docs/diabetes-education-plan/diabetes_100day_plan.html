1. MISSION, AUDIENCE, BRAND VOICE

Your Mission Statement (pin to your studio wall):

"I help Indian patients and their families understand diabetes through evidence — so they can choose lifestyle, medication and reversal pathways without fear or false promises."

Primary Audience:

  • Adults 28–60, newly diagnosed Type 2 diabetes or prediabetes
  • Family members of patients with Type 1 diabetes
  • People at high risk: BMI ≥ 25, family history, PCOS, gestational diabetes history
  • Pharmacists, nursing staff, junior doctors who refer to your channel

Brand Pillars:

  1. Evidence over emotion — every claim cites a study or guideline.
  2. Empathy first — talk with people, not down to them.
  3. Pharmacology you can feel — explain drugs by what they do in the body, not by brand name.
  4. Indian context — rice, roti, millets, chai, festivals, Ramadan fasting, shift workers.
  5. Honest about reversal — yes for many T2D, never for T1D.

Brand Voice Rules:

  • Speak in 8th-grade English / simple Hindi-English blend.
  • Never say "cure" — say "remission" and explain why.
  • Never name and shame other influencers — debunk the idea, not the person.
  • Always end with one specific action the viewer can do today.

DAY 2 — What is diabetes in one minute

Hook lines

  • "If you can read this sentence, your brain is running on glucose right now. Diabetes is what happens when that delivery system breaks."
  • "Diabetes is not a sugar disease. It is an insulin disease. Today's video changes how you see it."
  • "Your blood is supposed to have sugar in it. The problem starts when there is too much, for too long."

Instagram Reel script (75 seconds)

[0:00–0:05] Hook "Diabetes is not a sugar disease. It is an insulin disease."

[0:05–0:25] The simple version "Every cell in your body needs glucose for fuel. Glucose comes from food. To get into the cell, glucose needs a key called insulin, made by your pancreas.³"

[0:25–0:45] What goes wrong "In Type 1 diabetes, the body destroys the insulin factory. In Type 2, the lock on the cell becomes rusty — insulin is there but doesn't work well. Either way, sugar piles up in the blood instead of feeding the cell.⁴"

[0:45–1:10] Why it matters "That extra sugar damages your eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart and feet — slowly, silently, for years before you feel anything.⁵ This is why we treat the number before you feel the symptom."

[1:10–1:15] CTA "Tomorrow: how glucose and insulin actually talk to each other. Save and follow."

YouTube script (5 min)

[0:00–0:20] Cold open "You eat a roti. Within fifteen minutes, sugar from that roti is in your bloodstream. In a healthy body, it leaves the blood and enters your cells in under two hours. In diabetes, that journey breaks. Today we'll see exactly where it breaks."

[0:20–1:15] Where glucose comes from "Carbohydrates — rice, roti, dal, fruit, sugar, even milk — are broken down into glucose, a single sugar molecule. Your gut absorbs it into the blood. Your liver also makes glucose during fasting. So your blood always has some glucose: between 70 and 100 mg/dL when you wake up, between 100 and 140 after a meal.⁶"

[1:15–2:15] What insulin does "The pancreas, a leaf-shaped organ behind your stomach, has tiny clumps of cells called the islets of Langerhans. Inside the islets, beta-cells make insulin. Insulin is a hormone — a chemical messenger. When glucose rises in the blood after a meal, beta-cells release insulin. Insulin binds to a receptor on the surface of muscle, fat and liver cells, and triggers a transporter called GLUT4 to come to the surface and pull glucose in.⁷ Once glucose is inside the cell, it's burned for energy or stored. Blood glucose comes back down. That's the normal cycle."

[2:15–3:15] Type 1 — the factory is destroyed "In Type 1 diabetes, your own immune system — the same one that fights infections — mistakenly attacks the beta-cells. Over months to years, more than 80% of them are destroyed.⁸ Insulin production crashes. Without insulin, glucose can't enter cells, even though there's plenty of it in the blood. The body starts breaking down fat for fuel, producing ketones, and the patient ends up in diabetic ketoacidosis. Type 1 needs insulin from outside, for life."

[3:15–4:15] Type 2 — the locks get rusty "Type 2 is different. The factory still works at first — sometimes even overworks. The problem is the locks. Muscle, liver and fat cells stop responding properly to insulin; this is called insulin resistance. The pancreas tries to compensate by making more insulin. For years, this hides the problem. Eventually, the beta-cells get tired, and now you have insulin resistance plus falling insulin — that's full-blown Type 2 diabetes.⁹"

[4:15–5:00] Why we care about a number you can't feel "Here's the cruel part. You won't feel high blood sugar until it's been high for years. By the time symptoms hit — frequent urination, thirst, weight loss, blurred vision — the damage to small blood vessels has already started.¹⁰ This is why screening matters. This is why treating the number matters. Tomorrow we'll go deeper into the glucose-insulin handshake. See you then."

Hook lines

  • "Insulin is not the bad guy. Insulin is what keeps you alive after every meal."
  • "If insulin disappeared for 24 hours, you would not survive. Today's video shows you why."

Instagram Reel (90 sec)

[0:00–0:05] Hook "Insulin is not the bad guy. Insulin is what keeps you alive after every meal."

[0:05–0:30] What insulin actually does "Three jobs. One — opens muscle and fat cells so glucose can enter. Two — tells the liver to stop making more glucose. Three — tells the body to store extra fuel as glycogen and fat for later.¹¹"

[0:30–0:55] The myth "Social media says 'insulin makes you fat'. Truth — insulin manages the fuel you eat. Eat too much, of course you'll store fat. Insulin is the warehouse manager, not the thief."

[0:55–1:20] Why diabetics need it "In Type 1, you make zero. In late Type 2, you make too little. Without enough insulin, your blood is rich in glucose but your cells are starving. That's why people lose weight in uncontrolled diabetes."

[1:20–1:30] CTA "Tomorrow — meet the pancreas. Save this Reel."

YouTube (5 min 30 sec)

[0:00–0:20] Cold open "There's a viral idea online that insulin is the enemy. That you should reduce insulin to lose weight, to reverse diabetes, to live longer. Today I'll show you why that idea is half right and half dangerously wrong."

[0:20–1:30] What insulin really does "Insulin is a hormone made by the beta cells of the pancreas. Think of it as a postman delivering three messages. Message one, to muscle and fat cells: open your doors, glucose is here. Message two, to the liver: stop pouring out glucose, the meal has arrived. Message three, to the whole body: store the surplus — as glycogen in liver and muscle, and as fat in adipose tissue, for the next fasting period.¹¹ Without these three messages, glucose stays trapped in the blood, and the body literally starves in the middle of plenty."

[1:30–2:30] The fed state vs the fasting state "After a meal, insulin rises. Glucose falls. The body is in 'fed state' — storing energy. Between meals or overnight, insulin falls and another hormone, glucagon, takes over. Glucagon tells the liver to release stored glucose. This back and forth is how your blood sugar stays in the 70-to-140 mg/dL range no matter what you eat. In diabetes, the back and forth breaks. Either insulin doesn't come (Type 1), or it comes but the cells ignore it (Type 2)."¹²

[2:30–3:30] Why the 'insulin makes you fat' line is misleading "Yes, insulin promotes storage. But insulin only acts on what you eat. If you eat 1,500 calories, insulin manages 1,500 calories. The reason people gain weight in modern life is excess calories — especially refined carbohydrates and ultra-processed snacks — not insulin existing. Studies that compare low-carb to low-fat diets, when calories are matched, show very similar weight loss.¹³ The honest summary: insulin is a tool. Cutting refined carbs lowers insulin spikes, which is helpful for many T2D patients. But demonising the hormone itself is wrong."

[3:30–4:30] What happens without insulin "Imagine the postman is gone. Glucose arrives in the blood after a meal but cannot enter cells. The blood sugar climbs to 300, 400, 500 mg/dL. The cells, starving, send signals to the liver to make more glucose — which makes the blood sugar even higher. The body shifts to burning fat for fuel, producing ketones, which acidify the blood. This is diabetic ketoacidosis: nausea, vomiting, deep rapid breathing, confusion, coma. Without insulin, a Type 1 diabetic can be in DKA in less than 24 hours.¹⁴ This is why nobody — no influencer, no diet, no oil — can replace insulin in Type 1."

[4:30–5:30] What it means for you "If you are on insulin and someone tells you to stop because insulin is bad — please ignore them, then call your doctor before you do anything. If you are on metformin or other tablets, none of those tablets are insulin; they help your own pancreas or your own cells use insulin better. Tomorrow we'll go inside the pancreas itself. See you in the next one."

YouTube (5 min)

[0:00–0:20] Cold open "This organ weighs less than 100 grams. It is hidden behind your stomach. You cannot feel it. And yet, when it fails, an entire global industry has to keep you alive with injections. Meet the pancreas."

[0:20–1:15] Anatomy "The pancreas sits horizontally in the upper abdomen, behind the stomach, in front of the spine. It has a head — tucked into the curve of the duodenum — a body, and a tail that reaches towards the spleen. Its main duct empties into the small intestine, near the bile duct.¹⁵ It has two completely separate jobs. The exocrine pancreas, which is most of the gland, makes digestive enzymes that break down protein, fat and carbs in your gut. The endocrine pancreas — only 1 to 2% of the organ — is responsible for everything we discuss in diabetes."

[1:15–2:15] The Islets of Langerhans "Scattered through the pancreas are about a million tiny clusters called the Islets of Langerhans, named after the German pathologist who described them in 1869. Each islet contains several hormone-making cell types. Beta cells make insulin and account for about 60-80% of the islet. Alpha cells make glucagon. Delta cells make somatostatin. PP cells make pancreatic polypeptide. Together, they are a miniature endocrine factory.¹⁶"

[2:15–3:15] How beta cells decide "Beta cells have a sensor for glucose. When blood glucose rises, glucose enters the beta cell through GLUT2, gets metabolised, and produces ATP. ATP closes a potassium channel, the cell becomes electrically excited, calcium rushes in, and pre-formed insulin granules fuse with the cell membrane and dump their contents into the blood.¹⁸ This entire process happens within a few minutes of you eating. Sulphonylureas — the older diabetes tablets — work by closing that same potassium channel, forcing the cell to release insulin even if the glucose isn't high enough."

[3:15–4:15] How alpha cells decide "Alpha cells do the opposite. When blood glucose falls — between meals, during exercise, while you sleep — alpha cells secrete glucagon. Glucagon travels to the liver and tells it to break down glycogen, the body's stored sugar, and release glucose into the blood. In Type 2 diabetes, there is a strange and underappreciated problem: alpha cells become inappropriately active even after meals, secreting glucagon when they shouldn't. The liver dumps glucose right when you've just eaten. This is one of the eight defects in DeFronzo's 'Ominous Octet' theory of T2D.¹⁷"

[4:15–5:00] What this means in practice "This anatomy lecture has a practical point. It tells you why we use multiple drugs — because Type 2 diabetes isn't one defect. Metformin works on the liver and muscle. SGLT2 inhibitors work on the kidney. GLP-1 receptor agonists work on the alpha and beta cells, the brain and the gut. Insulin replaces what the beta cell can no longer make. There is no single magic pill — because there is no single broken part. Tomorrow, we'll talk about HbA1c — your blood's three-month report card. See you in the next one."

[0:20–1:30] What HbA1c actually measures "Inside every red blood cell is haemoglobin — the protein that carries oxygen. Glucose floating in your bloodstream slowly attaches itself to haemoglobin in a process called glycation. The longer glucose has been high, and the higher it has been, the more haemoglobin gets glycated. We measure the percentage of haemoglobin that is glycated — that's HbA1c. Because red blood cells live about 120 days, HbA1c reflects average glucose over the past 2 to 3 months.¹⁹"

[1:30–2:30] Reading the number "The American Diabetes Association cut-offs are: below 5.7% is normal, 5.7 to 6.4% is prediabetes, 6.5% or higher on two separate tests confirms diabetes.²⁰ The ICMR uses the same cut-offs in India. So when your doctor says 'HbA1c of 9 is bad', what she means is your average sugar for three months has been over 200."

[2:30–3:30] Why HbA1c matters more than a single fasting sugar "Fasting glucose is one snapshot. After you eat, your sugar can spike to 250 and come back down — and morning fasting will look fine. HbA1c sees those spikes. The UKPDS trial, which followed thousands of T2D patients for over a decade, showed that every 1 percentage-point reduction in HbA1c reduced microvascular complications — eyes, kidneys, nerves — by about 37%, and reduced heart attack risk by about 14%.²¹ So this number is not for your doctor's file. It is for your eyes, your kidneys, and your heart."

[3:30–4:30] When HbA1c lies "HbA1c is not perfect. Anything that changes the lifespan of red blood cells changes the test. If your red cells live shorter — for example in iron-deficiency anaemia after treatment, in haemolytic anaemia, in pregnancy, in chronic kidney disease, after blood loss — your HbA1c will read falsely low. If your red cells live longer — untreated iron deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, alcoholism — your HbA1c can read falsely high.²² India also has a high prevalence of haemoglobin variants like HbE, HbS and beta-thalassaemia trait, especially in eastern and southern states; these can interfere with certain HbA1c assays.²³ This is why we don't diagnose diabetes on HbA1c alone in such patients — we add a fasting sugar or an OGTT."

[4:30–5:30] How to use this number "If your HbA1c is in prediabetes range, you have a window. Recheck every 3 months if you're adjusting treatment, every 6 months if stable. Tomorrow we'll go even deeper — fasting, post-meal and random sugars, and what they actually tell your doctor. [0:20–1:20] Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) "FPG is measured after at least 8 hours without food — usually first thing in the morning before breakfast. Water is allowed. It tells us what your body, especially your liver, is doing in the absence of food. Diabetes: 200 or higher.²⁰ This number tends to rise before fasting glucose does. Many South Asians with normal fasting sugars already have impaired post-meal sugars — what's called isolated post-prandial hyperglycaemia.²⁵ This is why doctors often add an OGTT or HbA1c when the fasting test alone looks fine but the patient is high-risk."

[2:20–3:20] Random plasma glucose (RPG) "This is taken at any time of day, irrespective of meals. [3:20–4:00] What about glucometer readings at home? "A finger-prick glucometer is great for trends, not for diagnosis. Lab plasma readings are about 10 to 12% higher than capillary readings on the same person at the same time, because the meter measures whole blood. [4:00–5:00] Putting all three together "A complete picture of your glucose has three layers. Fasting tells me about your liver and overnight metabolism. DAY 71 — Diabetic retinopathy — save your eyes

Hooks: "You'll never feel diabetic retinopathy. Until you can't see your child's face." / "One in three Indian diabetics has retinopathy. Most don't know."

IG: Microvascular damage to retinal capillaries → microaneurysms → haemorrhages → neovascularisation → blindness if untreated.¹⁷³ Annual dilated fundus examination from diagnosis. Tight HbA1c and BP control prevent progression.

YT:

  • Stages — non-proliferative (mild/moderate/severe) → proliferative → diabetic macular edema.

  • Indian SN-DREAMS data on prevalence.¹⁷⁵
  • Tele-ophthalmology screening.

DAY 75 — Heart attack risk in diabetes

Hooks: "A diabetic without prior MI has the same heart-attack risk as a non-diabetic with prior MI." / "Statins, blood pressure, glucose, lifestyle — together cut CV death in half."

IG: Haffner et al. landmark study: T2D patients without prior MI had similar 7-year MI risk as non-diabetics with prior MI.¹⁸³ Steno-2 — multifactorial intervention ↓ all-cause mortality 46% over 21 years.¹⁸⁴

  • ABCs of CV protection — A1c < 7, BP < 130/80, LDL < 70 in high-risk, smoking cessation.

  • Aspirin only in secondary prevention or selected high-risk.

DAY 76 — Stroke and diabetes

Hooks: "Diabetes doubles your stroke risk and triples your stroke severity." / "BP < 130/80 + statin + glucose < 7% = the cheapest stroke vaccine you'll ever buy."

IG: Emerging Risk Factors Collaboration: T2D ↑ ischaemic stroke risk ~2.3×.¹⁸⁵ Atrial fibrillation more common in T2D.

YT:

  • Recognising stroke — FAST.

DAY 77 — Diabetes and gum disease

IG: Periodontitis prevalence 2× higher in T2D; treatment of periodontitis ↓ HbA1c ~0.4%.¹⁸⁶ Regular dental cleaning every 6 months.

IG: Acanthosis nigricans (insulin resistance), diabetic dermopathy (shin spots), necrobiosis lipoidica, granuloma annulare, candidal intertrigo.¹⁸⁷ Often a first clue.

DAY 79 — Sexual health and diabetes

PHASE 9 — SPECIAL POPULATIONS (DAYS 81–90)

DAY 81 — Diabetes in pregnancy — beyond GDM

Hooks: "Pre-existing diabetes + pregnancy = a different game. The first trimester decides the baby's organs." / "Every woman with diabetes who could become pregnant should be on folate today."

IG (90 s): Pre-pregnancy HbA1c < 6.5% halves congenital malformation risk.¹⁹⁰ Switch to insulin pre-conception (most oral agents excluded). Folic acid 5 mg/day from 3 months pre-pregnancy.

YT (5–6 min):

  • Pre-conception counselling for T1D and T2D.
  • Trimester targets: fasting < 95, 1 h pp < 140, 2 h pp < 120 mg/dL.
  • Insulin requirements rise 2–3× by 3rd trimester; review fortnightly.
  • Retinopathy and nephropathy may worsen — pre-pregnancy screening mandatory.
  • Postpartum: insulin needs crash within 24 h — risk of hypos.

DAY 82 — Type 1 diabetes in children

Hooks: "A 6-year-old wetting the bed and losing weight — please test today, not next week." / "T1D in children is a medical emergency disguised as flu."

IG (90 s): Onset peak ages 4–6 and 10–14. Classic features — polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, fatigue, fruity breath, vomiting (DKA).¹⁹¹ Test fingerprick glucose + ketones in any unwell child with these features.

  • Indian incidence: 4 per 100,000 children/year, rising.¹⁹²

DAY 83 — Type 2 diabetes in teenagers — the new epidemic

IG (90 s): Indian adolescent T2D rising sharply with childhood obesity (~14% in metros). Risk factors: BMI ≥ 95th percentile, acanthosis nigricans, family history, PCOS, GDM-exposed in utero.¹⁹³

DAY 84 — Diabetes in the elderly — different rules

DAY 85 — Diabetes and Ramadan fasting

PHASE 10 — MISINFORMATION AND YOUR ACTION PLAN (DAYS 91–100)

DAY 91 — The science of testimonials — why they mislead

Hooks: "Survivor bias, regression to the mean, honeymoon effect, placebo. The four reasons testimonials lie — even the honest ones." / "For every reversal video, ten relapse videos are never posted."

IG (90 s): Cognitive traps in testimonial reasoning: (1) selection bias — only success stories shared; (2) regression to the mean — outliers normalise on their own; (3) confounding — diet + exercise + drug + life event happen together; (4) placebo effect; (5) survivor bias.²⁰²

YT (5–6 min):

  • Hierarchy of evidence revisited.
  • Why an n=1 anecdote ≠ proof, but ≠ false either.
  • Practical questions to ask before believing any "I cured my diabetes" video: type? duration? medications? weight loss? labs? follow-up years?
  • Real Indian remission data (DiRECT-style cohorts).¹⁶²

Refs:

  1. Sackett DL. Bias in analytic research. J Chronic Dis. 1979;32(1-2):51-63.

DAY 92 — "He reversed Type 1 with millets" — debunked

Hooks: "If a video claims to reverse Type 1 diabetes — close it. Right now." / "There has never been a peer-reviewed paper showing reversal of confirmed Type 1 diabetes by any diet."

IG (90 s): Real T1D = autoimmune destruction of β-cells. Confirmed by GAD/IA-2/insulin autoantibodies, low fasting C-peptide.⁴⁹ Apparent "reversal" is almost always: (a) honeymoon period; (b) misdiagnosed T2D/LADA/MODY; (c) outright fabrication.

YT (5–6 min):

  • Walk-through of how to evaluate a "Type 1 reversal" claim.
  • The biology — once β-cells are destroyed, no diet regenerates them.
  • Stem-cell trials (VX-880) — what they show and what they don't.¹⁷¹
  • A polite, firm script for family members who push diet "cures" on a T1 child.

Refs: 49, 52, 171.

DAY 93 — Coconut oil and diabetes — the real story

Hooks: "Coconut oil is fine. 'Coconut oil reverses diabetes' is not." / "It is 90% saturated fat. Whatever the influencer told you, your LDL agrees with me."

IG (90 s): Khaw et al. RCT — virgin coconut oil raised LDL more than olive oil, similar to butter.²⁰³ AHA 2017 advisory — saturated fats raise LDL, increase CV risk.²⁰⁴ Use in moderation (1–2 tsp/day), not as a "medicine".

YT (5–6 min):

  • Saturated vs MUFA vs PUFA basics.
  • MCT oil ≠ coconut oil (only ~14% MCT in coconut).
  • Indian cooking oils ranked: mustard, sunflower, groundnut, rice bran — rotation strategy.
  • Where the "metabolic boost" claim actually came from.
